Laterally Loaded Panels: Theory

This chapter explains the flexural-strength route for wind-loaded masonry panels. It focuses on support conditions, orthogonal ratio, bending moment coefficients, moment resistance and the effect of openings.

9.1 Purpose and learning outcomes

Laterally loaded panel design is mainly used for wind-loaded wall panels. Earth-retaining cases should not normally rely on masonry flexural tensile strength alone.

Support condition

Decide whether each edge is free, simply supported or continuous before selecting moment coefficients.

Strength direction

Masonry has different flexural strengths depending on whether failure is parallel or perpendicular to bed joints.

Opening judgement

Openings, DPCs and movement joints can change the effective structural panel and the assumed support condition.

9.2 Design route for laterally loaded panels

Eurocode 6 allows laterally loaded panels to be checked using a flexural-strength method with bending moment coefficients, or by arching action where rigid supports can resist arch thrust. The flexural route is the usual teaching route because it does not require arch thrust resistance at the supports.

1 · GeometryPanel height, length, thickness and support edges.
2 · LoadDesign wind pressure WEd.
3 · CoefficientMoment coefficient α from support pattern.
4 · ResistanceCheck MEd ≤ MRd.
MEd = α WEd l2

For a simple one-way simply supported panel, α = 1/8. For two-way spanning panels, α depends on aspect ratio, edge support and the orthogonal ratio.

9.3 Orthogonal ratio and flexural strength

Masonry is anisotropic. The flexural tensile strength depends on the direction of the failure plane relative to the bed joints. This is why laterally loaded panel design must distinguish between fxk1 and fxk2.

fxk1 — failure parallel to bed joints

Often critical for vertical one-way spanning wall panels, because cracking can follow the bed joints.

fxk2 — failure perpendicular to bed joints

Often relevant when the panel spans horizontally and cracking passes through units and joints.

μ = fxk1fxk2

The orthogonal ratio is used to select appropriate two-way bending moment coefficients. For real design, use the coefficient framework and material values required by the project National Annex.

9.4 Moment resistance

The design flexural strength is obtained by dividing the characteristic flexural strength by the relevant material partial factor. The wall strip moment resistance is then calculated using the section modulus.

fxd = fxkγM
Z = bt26
MRd = fxdZ
MEd ≤ MRd

If vertical compression is present and the failure plane is parallel to bed joints, the apparent flexural resistance may be increased by the design vertical compressive stress, but only within the EC6 limits and with correct detailing assumptions.

9.5 Support conditions and openings

The engineer must decide whether each panel edge is free, simply supported or continuous. Details such as DPCs, movement joints, bonded returns and openings can change this assumption.

Free edge

No reliable moment or reaction restraint should be assumed along this edge.

Simple support

Rotation is allowed, but the edge can provide lateral support.

Continuous edge

Only assume continuity where the actual construction detail can transfer the required action.

Opening subdivision concept

Large or irregular openings should not be forced into a simple rectangular panel without engineering judgement. Subdivision, yield-line analysis or FEM may be more reliable.

9.6 Chapter summary

Design flow

  • Start with support condition, panel geometry and design wind pressure.
  • Use the correct bending moment coefficient for the panel behaviour.
  • Compare MEd with MRd.

Engineering judgement

  • Flexural resistance depends on failure direction relative to bed joints.
  • Openings and movement joints can redefine the panel.
  • Irregular panels may require recognised direct analysis.

Common mistakes to flag

Using the wrong flexural strength direction, especially mixing up fxk1 and fxk2.
Assuming all edges are continuous without checking the actual wall/floor/detail connection.
Ignoring DPCs, movement joints or poor junctions when selecting support conditions.
Treating a wall with a large opening as a single full rectangular panel without checking subdivision.
Using characteristic wind pressure instead of the design lateral action.
Applying masonry flexural tensile strength to earth-retaining cases without an appropriate design method.
