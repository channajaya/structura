Chapter position and purpose

This chapter explains the design framework used throughout the book. The reader learns how characteristic loads are converted into design actions and how those actions are compared with design resistance. Later chapters then use the same framework for wall, column, bearing and lateral-load checks.

1. Limit State Design

Eurocode design is based on limit states. The Ultimate Limit State checks safety against collapse or serious failure. The Serviceability Limit State checks normal use, including cracking, movement, deflection and appearance.

The basic design condition is:

$$E_d \le R_d$$
ULSSafety

Checks collapse, crushing, instability, shear failure and other serious resistance failures.

SLSUsability

Checks whether the structure remains acceptable in normal use.

NDPNational Annex

Provides country-specific values where Eurocodes allow national choice.

EN 1990 Section 2EN 1996-1-1 §2.1–2.2

2. Actions on Masonry Structures

Actions are loads or imposed effects applied to the structure. For masonry walls, they usually include self-weight, floor and roof loads, imposed loads and wind loads.

\(G_k\)Permanent actions

Self-weight, finishes and fixed elements that remain during the life of the structure.

\(Q_k\)Variable actions

Imposed floor load, wind load, snow load and other actions that may vary.

\(A_d\)Accidental actions

Impact, explosion or other abnormal events considered where relevant.

3. ULS Combination of Actions

For a simple persistent ULS case with one permanent action and one leading variable action, the teaching form is:

$$E_d = \gamma_G G_k + \gamma_Q Q_k$$

Typical teaching values are \(\gamma_G=1.35\) for unfavourable permanent action and \(\gamma_Q=1.50\) for the leading variable action. In real projects, EN 1990 and the project National Annex must be checked.

Important: This simplified form is only for teaching one permanent action plus one leading variable action. Multiple variable actions, favourable actions, accidental combinations and serviceability combinations need the full EN 1990 route.
EN 1990 §6.4.3

4. Pattern Loading

Pattern loading is used where the position of the variable load can create the worst moment, reaction or eccentricity. The permanent load remains present, while the variable load is placed in the position giving the worst design effect.

Outer-span variable loading is commonly checked when it may create a worse support effect.

  • Loaded span: use the leading variable action where it gives the worst effect.
  • Unloaded span: take only permanent load, or compare with other arrangements where required.
  • This matters because floor reactions can create eccentric moments in masonry walls.

5. Material Partial Factors

The material partial factor reduces characteristic material strength to a design value. It reflects uncertainty in unit quality, mortar quality and execution control.

$$f_d = \frac{fk}{\gamma_M}$$

The value of \(\gamma_M\) depends on manufacturing category, execution control class and the stress state being checked.

6. General Masonry Design Workflow

The following sequence is the general route used in later worked examples. It connects actions from EN 1990/EN 1991 with masonry resistance from EN 1996.

Determine the characteristic actions: \(G_k\), \(Q_k\) and any other relevant actions.
Combine actions to obtain the design action effect \(E_d\).
Select unit and mortar properties.
Calculate material strengths such as \(fk\) and \(f_d\).
Determine geometry, effective height and effective thickness.
Calculate eccentricity and any reduction factors.
Calculate the design resistance \(R_d\).
Check \(E_d \le R_d\).
EN 1996-1-1 §2.4

9. Common Mistakes to Flag

Using characteristic loads directly as design actions.
Using \(\gamma_M\) from the wrong stress state or control category.
Forgetting pattern loading when variable load position affects wall moment.
Mixing values from different National Annexes.
