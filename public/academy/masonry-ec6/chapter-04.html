Mechanics of Masonry Behaviour

Compression, flexure, shear and anisotropy — the physical behaviour behind the strength values and reduction factors used in later masonry design checks.

Chapter position and purpose

This chapter explains why masonry behaves differently in compression, flexure and shear. The aim is to help the reader understand the failure mechanism before applying EC6 equations in later chapters.

compressionStrong direction

Vertical compression is the main strength of masonry, but failure is controlled by unit-mortar interaction.

tensionWeak direction

Unreinforced masonry has low direct tensile strength and cracks easily when pulled.

flexureDirection dependent

Flexural strength changes depending on whether the failure plane is parallel or perpendicular to bed joints.

shearBond + friction

Shear resistance comes from bed-joint bond and friction caused by vertical pre-compression.

1. Compression Behaviour

EN 1996-1-1 §3.6.1 treats masonry compressive strength as a property of the assembled wall, not the brick or block alone. Under vertical compression, mortar usually deforms laterally more than the masonry units. The units restrain this lateral movement, which introduces tensile stresses in the units.

Concept diagram — unit-mortar interaction under compression

Failure may therefore appear as vertical splitting or cracking through the units rather than simple crushing of the mortar.

  • Unit strength has a strong influence on \(fk\).
  • Mortar strength has an influence, but usually a smaller one.
  • Thick or poorly filled mortar joints can reduce strength.
  • Good bedding and workmanship are needed for the design assumptions to remain valid.
$$ fk = K fb^{\alpha} fm^{\beta} $$

2. Tensile and Flexural Behaviour

EN 1996-1-1 §3.6.3 distinguishes two flexural tensile strengths because the bed joints make masonry direction-dependent. This is one of the most important ideas for laterally loaded wall panels.

Failure plane parallel to bed joints

This direction is represented by \(f_{xk1}\). It is commonly associated with bond failure along bed joints and usually gives a lower flexural strength than the perpendicular case.

$$ f_{xk1}:\; \text{failure plane parallel to bed joints} $$
Use the project National Annex for actual flexural strength values. The table images are provided only as reference aids in the pop-up windows.

3. Shear Behaviour

EN 1996-1-1 §3.6.2 and §6.2 relate shear resistance to bond between unit and mortar plus friction from vertical pre-compression.

Concept diagram — bed-joint shear resisted by bond and friction
$$ f_{vk} = f_{vk0} + 0.4\sigma_d \quad \text{subject to EC6 upper limits} $$

Pre-compression improves shear resistance, but the value cannot increase without limit because the failure mode may change to diagonal cracking or unit failure.

4. Anisotropy

Masonry is anisotropic because its properties depend on the direction of loading relative to the bed joints. The same wall can have different strengths in vertical compression, flexure parallel to bed joints, flexure perpendicular to bed joints and shear.

Loading caseTypical failure modeGoverning property
Vertical compressionUnit splitting or crushing\(fk\)
Flexure parallel to bed jointsBond failure along bed joints\(f_{xk1}\)
Flexure perpendicular to bed jointsCracking through units and joints\(f_{xk2}\)
In-plane shearBed-joint sliding or stepped cracking\(f_{vk}\)
$$ \mu = \frac{f_{xk1}}{f_{xk2}} $$

The orthogonal ratio \(\mu\) is used later in lateral-load design to account for different flexural strengths in the two principal masonry directions.

5. FEM-01 Link

A useful FEM learning example for this chapter is a small unit-mortar-unit compression model. The output can show lateral tensile stress developing in the unit when the softer mortar tries to expand laterally.

Software suggestion
Abaqus/CAE with Abaqus/Standard.
Model type
Two unit blocks with one mortar bed. Start with linear elastic properties, then extend to damage material if needed.
Expected output
Stress contours showing lateral tension in the masonry unit and high compression in the mortar bed.
This FEM link is not a design requirement. It is a visual learning exercise that supports the compression mechanism explained in Section 1.

7. Common Mistakes to Flag

Assuming masonry is isotropicMasonry strength changes with loading direction relative to bed joints.
Using direct tensile strength incorrectlyFor lateral wall design, flexural strength values are normally required.
Ignoring vertical pre-compressionPre-compression can improve shear resistance and lateral flexural behaviour.
Thinking mortar strength alone controls compressionCompression behaviour depends on unit strength, mortar, bond and workmanship.
