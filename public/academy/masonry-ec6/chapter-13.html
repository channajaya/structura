Combined Vertical + Lateral Loading

This chapter shows how to organise masonry checks when axial compression, eccentricity and lateral wind action act on the same wall or pier.

EN 1996-1-1 §6.1.2§6.3 + §6.4Annex GWE-11

Learning focus

  • Separate vertical and flexural checks.
  • Use eccentricity and reduction factors correctly.
  • Use precompression only where permitted.
  • Read combined loading as an interaction problem.

13.1 Purpose of this chapter

Many real masonry members do not carry only one action. A loadbearing wall or pier may carry vertical floor or roof loads while also resisting wind pressure. A column may also receive vertical load with eccentricity and a lateral load acting in one or both directions.

Helpful effect

Vertical compression can increase the apparent flexural resistance for bending that causes tension parallel to bed joints.

Harmful effect

Vertical load with eccentricity reduces vertical resistance through the capacity reduction factor Φ.

Design decision

Both effects must be checked carefully and must not be double-counted.

Clean teaching diagram: axial load, eccentricity and lateral wind action on a masonry pier.

13.2 What combined loading means

Combined loading means that the same masonry element is checked under vertical compression and bending action at the same time. The bending may be caused by eccentric bearing, wind pressure, horizontal frame action, construction tolerance, initial eccentricity or biaxial bending.

Vertical resistance check

Confirm that the member can carry the design vertical load after allowing for slenderness and eccentricity.

Lateral/flexural resistance check

Confirm that the bending moment caused by lateral load is resisted by the masonry section, with any allowable benefit from vertical compression.

Important: the same vertical load can reduce vertical resistance through eccentricity and also improve some flexural checks through precompression. Treat these as separate effects.

13.3 Eurocode 6 design routes

Route A — Stability/eccentricity route: convert wind or lateral action into an additional moment or eccentricity, then check vertical resistance using the relevant reduction factor.
Route B — Flexural strength route with precompression: treat the wall as a laterally loaded panel and include vertical compression as an increase in apparent design flexural strength where permitted.
Route C — Interaction approach: check the design point against an interaction surface or diagram. This is useful for biaxial bending, high eccentricity and FEM comparison.

13.4 Key equations used in this chapter

These equations organise the worked example. In real projects, National Annex values and project-specific restrictions must be verified.

Vertical resistance

NRd = ΦAfd

Φ reduces resistance for slenderness and eccentricity.

Design compressive strength

fd = fk/γM

Use the correct partial factor for the masonry and execution assumptions.

Eccentricity

e = MEd/NEd + einit

Eccentricity may come from moment, load position and initial imperfections.

Flexural resistance

MRd = fxdZ

For a rectangular section, use the appropriate section modulus for the bending direction.

Precompression benefit

fxd1,app = fxd1 + σd

The added compression term must stay within the permitted EC6 limits.

One-way lateral bending

MEd = αWEdl²

α is the bending coefficient, WEd is design pressure, and l is the relevant span.

13.5 Biaxial bending and interaction diagram concept

Biaxial bending occurs when the member bends about both principal directions. This commonly happens in piers and columns because vertical load may be eccentric in two directions and wind may act in either the major or minor axis direction.

Clean interaction concept: the design point should remain inside the capacity boundary.

13.8 Chapter summary

Key points

  • Separate combined loading into vertical resistance, flexural resistance and interaction review.
  • Eccentricity and slenderness reduce vertical resistance.
  • Vertical compression may improve lateral flexural resistance only within permitted limits.
  • Biaxial bending should be checked in both principal directions.

Common mistakes

Checking only the wind moment and forgetting the vertical resistance check.
Double-counting the benefit of vertical compression.
Using one axis section modulus for both bending directions.
Ignoring initial eccentricity and Annex G reduction factors.
