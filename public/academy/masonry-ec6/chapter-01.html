Chapter position

Purpose of this chapter

This chapter introduces masonry as a structural material and gives the basic vocabulary needed before the strength calculations and design checks are introduced in later chapters.

Main idea: masonry design is based on the behaviour of the completed wall — units, mortar, bond and workmanship acting together — not on the brick or block strength alone.

Vocabulary first

These terms appear throughout the book. Understanding them early makes later equations and design checks easier to follow.

Behaviour

2. Masonry as a structural material

Masonry is an assembly of individual units laid in a regular bond and joined with mortar. It is a composite material because the structural behaviour comes from the combined action of the units, mortar, bond pattern and workmanship.

  • strong in compression;
  • weak in direct tension;
  • direction-dependent, or anisotropic;
  • sensitive to eccentric loading and slenderness;
  • highly dependent on construction quality.

Key point
Masonry is an assembly of units bound with mortar: strong in compression and weak in direct tension. See EN 1996-1-1 §1 for the governing definitions.
Applications

3. Structural uses of masonry

Masonry can be used as a primary load-bearing system or as part of another structural system. In low-rise buildings, walls may carry gravity loads from floors and roofs. In framed buildings, masonry may act as cladding or infill and may mainly resist out-of-plane wind load.

W

Load-bearing walls and piers

Carry vertical floor or roof loads and sometimes lateral actions.

C

Columns, arches and vaults

Use compressive action and geometry to transfer loads.

P

Panels and cladding

Cavity walls, veneer walls and infill panels often need support-condition checks.

Key point
Common structural forms include walls, columns, arches and vaults, built in stone, clay-brick, concrete-block or reinforced masonry.
Materials

4. Main masonry unit types

The type of unit affects strength, density, durability, dimensions, mortar selection and the coefficient used to calculate characteristic compressive strength.

Key point
Typical unit types include fired-clay bricks, concrete masonry units and autoclaved aerated concrete (AAC) blocks; unit specifications are given in the EN 771 series.
Wall build-up

5. Bonding and wall construction forms

Bonding means arranging the units so that vertical joints are staggered and the wall behaves as one structural element. Poor bonding can create vertical planes of weakness.

The main construction forms are single-leaf walls, cavity walls, double-leaf walls, veneer walls and reinforced masonry. Each form changes how loads are transferred and how the wall is checked.
Key point
Common bonds — stretcher, English and Flemish — control load transfer and crack behaviour across the wall.
Design framework

6. Eurocode 6 in the design system

Eurocode 6 is used together with other Eurocodes. gives the basis of design and load combinations. gives actions such as imposed load and wind. gives material, analysis and resistance rules for masonry.

DocumentRole in masonry design
EN 1990Basis of design, limit states and action combinations.
EN 1991Actions on structures, including imposed loads and wind.
EN 1996-1-1General rules for reinforced and unreinforced masonry.
EN 1996-1-2Structural fire design.
EN 1996-2Selection of materials, detailing and execution.
EN 1996-3Simplified methods for unreinforced masonry.
EN 1990Safety, serviceability and robustness.
EN 1991Loads applied to the structure.
EN 1996Design and detailing of masonry structures.
Key point
EN 1990 sets the basis of structural design, EN 1991 supplies the actions, and EN 1996 (Eurocode 6) gives the masonry design rules — see EN 1990 §1 and EN 1996-1-1 §1.

Design warnings

8. Common mistakes to flag

Using brick strength directly.
The wall has its own masonry strength, influenced by unit, mortar and workmanship.
Forgetting weak tension.
Masonry is strong in compression but weak in direct tension.
Ignoring bond pattern.
Poor bond can create vertical weakness and poor crack control.
Misclassifying wall role.
Do not treat veneer or infill panels as primary load-bearing walls without checking support conditions.
Skipping National Annex checks.
Eurocode 6 values can depend on the relevant National Annex.
